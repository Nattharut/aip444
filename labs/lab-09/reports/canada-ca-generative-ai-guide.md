# Credibility Report: Guide on the use of generative artificial intelligence

**URL:** https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai/generative-ai/guide-use-generative-ai.html
**Verdict:** High credibility
**Evaluated:** 2026-11-26

## Summary

This is the Government of Canada's official guide for federal institutions on using generative AI, published on Canada.ca by the Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat (TBS). It explains what generative AI is, sets out the TBS "FASTER" principles (Fair, Accountable, Secure, Transparent, Educated, Relevant), and gives policy considerations, best practices and FAQ answers for public servants and for institutions deploying tools to the public. Its main claims are corroborated by other Government of Canada pages, independent news coverage of the guidelines' release, and external technical/scientific sources (OpenAI developer documentation on model training cut-offs; the IEA on data-centre energy use). The tone is neutral and it candidly lists risks to privacy, human rights, intellectual property, workers and the environment, so there is no evident bias beyond a natural institutional interest in promoting its own framework. The main shortcomings are that no individual author is named, the page's "Date modified" footer did not render for me (metadata shows 28 October 2026), and as a policy document it is not an independent scientific authority on AI's technical impacts — cite the underlying studies for those.

## Author

**No named author.** There is no byline, author box or "Author:" metadata field on the page. What the page does provide:

- The document sits in TBS's digital-government / responsible-use-of-AI section of Canada.ca.
- The "Additional support" section names **"TBS's Responsible Data and AI team"** with the contact email `ai-ia@tbs-sct.gc.ca`, and lists the Office of the Chief Information Officer of Canada (part of TBS) as a support body.
- The Guide says it "incorporates feedback from internal stakeholders and external experts," and that this is the "second version."

I checked: the byline area, the page metadata, the bottom-of-page material I could retrieve, and the parent pages (`responsible-use-ai.html`, `generative-ai.html`) for an author or team attribution. No individual's name, title or credentials appear anywhere. For a government policy instrument this is normal — the institution is the accountable author — but it means there is no individual expertise to verify and no named person to hold responsible for errors.

## Publication

**Government of Canada / Canada.ca, under the Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat.** Canada.ca is the official federal web domain, and TBS is the central agency that issues federal policy instruments. The Guide itself links to, and claims to support compliance with, instruments hosted on the TBS site: the *Policy on Service and Digital*, the *Directive on Service and Digital*, and the *Directive on Automated Decision-Making*. It also directs readers to TBS's *Procedures for Publishing*, which indicates the page goes through the government's formal publishing and review controls rather than being self-published.

Independent evidence of track record and process:

- TBS's own "Progress on AI in government" page records the release of the Guide and describes it as putting forward the FASTER principles and policy considerations.
- A TBS news release on the first federal AI strategy for the public service links the Guide as a resource for public servants.
- Mainstream and trade outlets covered the guidelines when they were issued: Global Government Forum, Radio-Canada (RCI), and CBC all report on the federal guidance for public servants using generative AI.
- Public-service union representatives (PIPSC president Jennifer Carr) publicly criticised the guidance as too vague and lacking clear "go/ no-go zones" — a critique of policy adequacy, not a factual correction.

I did not find any retraction, correction or misinformation finding about this page. Note that I could not render the printed footer ("Date modified") or the text of the 33 numbered footnotes with my reader; the in-text footnote markers and links do exist.

## Bias and Tone

- **Language:** neutral, formal, instructional — the register of government policy, not marketing or advocacy journalism.
- **Purpose:** to inform public servants and institutions and to set expectations, not to sell anything. No advertising; the only promotional links are to Government of Canada services (Canada School of Public Service courses, Canadian Centre for Cyber Security guidance).
- **Balance:** unusually candid. It devotes separate sections to inaccuracy/hallucination, bias, quality, public-servant autonomy and automation bias, legal risks, distinguishing humans from machines, and environmental impacts. It warns readers repeatedly *not* to treat outputs as authoritative and *not* to use these tools where factual accuracy is required.
- **Interests:** the Government of Canada has an institutional interest in promoting its own framework, and the document is by nature a statement of government policy — that is "advocacy" only in the sense that every policy instrument is. No commercial interest, no donation mechanism.
- **Missing:** it does not engage directly with critics who say the guidance is too vague; that debate appears in outside coverage (Global Government Forum, Radio-Canada), not on the page.

## Currency

- The page's machine-readable metadata gives a published/modified timestamp of **Wednesday, 28 October 2026** — about one month before this evaluation, so it is current.
- The printed footer "Date modified" did not render in my reader, so I am relying on the metadata timestamp.
- The Guide describes itself as the **second version** and says it "will be updated regularly to keep pace with regulatory and technological change."
- One sign of age in the content: it still uses "ChatGPT-3.5 is trained on data up to early 2022" as its example of stale training data, and discusses tools like GitHub Copilot/FauxPilot and DALL-E/Midjourney/Stable Diffusion as the examples. The framework is current, but individual examples may date quickly — check for a newer revision if you need the very latest tool landscape.

## Can I cite this?

**Yes — cite it as is**, with a couple of sensible precautions.

- **Best use:** any claim about *what the Government of Canada's guidance says*, federal AI policy, the FASTER principles, or the Canadian government's position on risks and best practices for generative AI. This is the authoritative primary document; there is no better source for that.
- **Cite with care:** for general factual claims about generative AI (bias, hallucination, language-performance gaps, environmental impacts, labour practices). The Guide is summarising other people's research, and its footnote text did not render for me. Cite the underlying study or dataset (e.g., the IEA *Energy and AI* report for energy/water figures) alongside or instead of the Guide for a stronger paper.
- **Note the vintage of examples:** if you quote the "ChatGPT-3.5 trained on data up to early 2022" line, flag that this was already imprecise (OpenAI's documented GPT-3.5 cutoff was September 2021) and that both the model and the example are now dated.
- **Do not use it** as evidence that Canadian AI guidance *works*, is *sufficient*, or is uncontested — that is an evaluative question, and unions and experts have publicly argued the guidance is too vague. Pair it with independent analysis/criticism (Global Government Forum, Radio-Canada) if your paper makes an argument about policy effectiveness.
- **Citation form:** Government of Canada, Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat. *Guide on the use of generative artificial intelligence.* Canada.ca, modified 28 October 2026. URL. (No individual author to cite; treat it as a corporate author.)
