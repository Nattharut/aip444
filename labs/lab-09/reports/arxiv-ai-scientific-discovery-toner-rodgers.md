# Credibility Report: Artificial Intelligence, Scientific Discovery, and Product Innovation

**URL:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.17866v1
**Verdict:** Very low credibility
**Evaluated:** 2026-11-26

## Summary

This is an arXiv preprint (arXiv:2412.17866v1, econ.GN, posted 21 December 2024) by Aidan Toner-Rodgers, then a PhD student in MIT's economics department. It reported that an AI materials-discovery tool rolled out at one anonymous U.S. firm's R&D lab raised materials discoveries by 44%, patent filings by 39% and product prototypes by 17%, and widened inequality between top and bottom scientists. It was never peer reviewed and its data came from a confidential single-firm setting that outside researchers could not check. In May 2025 MIT's Committee on Discipline concluded that MIT has "no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data" or "in the veracity of the research", asked arXiv and the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* to withdraw it, and stated the author is no longer at MIT; arXiv administrators then withdrew the paper "due to concerns about the validity of the data and incomplete Institutional Review Board requirements." Its large media footprint (Economist, ECB, Congressional Research Service, apparently a European Commission speech) is repetition of the preprint's own numbers, not independent corroboration.

## Author

- **Byline:** Aidan Toner-Rodgers, "MIT Department of Economics", aidantr@mit.edu (PDF title page).
- **Status at time of writing:** PhD student in MIT economics (MIT's statement calls him "a former second-year PhD student in the Department of Economics at MIT"). The paper was submitted to the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* but not published there.
- **Acknowledgements:** senior economists Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, Jacob Moscona and Nina Roussille, plus a long list of economists — which gave the preprint a credible-looking lineage.
- **Status now:** MIT states "The author is no longer at MIT." No peer-reviewed publications by this author on this topic were found.
- **Assessment:** A named author with a real institutional affiliation, but the institution that employed him has publicly disavowed the research's data and findings. His credentials cannot be used to support trust in this source.

## Publication

- **Host:** arXiv — a legitimate, widely used preprint server operated by Cornell, but it performs moderation, not peer review, and hosting implies no endorsement.
- **Current record:** the abstract page (https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17866) reads "This paper has been withdrawn by arXiv Admin" with the comment: *"Withdrawn by arXiv administrators due to concerns about the validity of the data and incomplete Institutional Review Board requirements."* Submission history shows v1 (21 Dec 2024) and v2 (20 May 2025, withdrawn); no PDF is offered from the abstract page.
- **Institutional action:** MIT contacted arXiv and the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* to request withdrawal, noting the paper "should be withdrawn from public discourse."
- **Editorial process:** none beyond arXiv moderation — effectively self-published preprint.

**Note on "contradicted":** the contradiction is not a competing estimate of the same effect. It is a finding by the author's own institution, plus arXiv's administrators, that the data and the research cannot be trusted — which is a credible source saying the reported results should not be relied on.

## Bias and Tone

The prose is calm, technical, hedged economics writing, and it even reports a negative consequence (82% of scientists less satisfied). That surface neutrality is exactly why the paper spread. But the substance is one-directional advocacy for AI-augmented research: every stage of R&D improves, novelty rises at all three stages, and the sole "cost" is subjective satisfaction. It relies on one anonymous corporate lab, provides no public data or replication package, and offers no serious discussion of what could produce such unusually clean effects. The author had a strong career incentive for a striking result; the paper was submitted to a top journal and heavily promoted in the press. There is no product being sold, but there is an agenda (AI complements expertise) and a reputational stake.

## Currency

- Posted to arXiv 21 December 2024 (PDF title page dated 25 December 2024); withdrawn 20 May 2025.
- As of 2026-11-26 the problem is not staleness but invalidity: this is a withdrawn preprint whose findings MIT says should not be relied on. It can only be cited as a case study in research misconduct/failed replication, not as evidence about AI and science.

## Can I cite this?

**Do not cite it for its findings.** A student who cites the 44% / 39% / 17% figures, or "AI doubles top researchers' output," is citing a withdrawn paper whose data MIT says it has no confidence in. If your instructor or a fact-checker looks it up, the arXiv page now says "withdrawn by arXiv administrators."

Legitimate uses are narrow and must be framed as such:

- **As a documented case study in research integrity**: "In 2024–25 a high-profile MIT preprint on AI and scientific discovery was widely covered and then withdrawn after MIT's Committee on Discipline found it could not vouch for the data; arXiv withdrew it in May 2025." Cite the MIT Economics statement and the arXiv record as your sources, and cite this preprint only as the object of the case, not as evidence about AI.
- **As an example of how quickly unreviewed preprints enter policy discourse**: the Science|Business report on a European Commission research commissioner appearing to cite it is a documented illustration. Again, cite the reporting, not the paper.

For actual evidence on AI and scientific productivity, find a better source: peer-reviewed studies, published replications, or systematic reviews indexed in EconLit/JSTOR/Scopus. If you want media coverage of AI's effects on research, prefer outlets that independently checked the numbers, and note whether the piece predates or postdates the May 2025 withdrawal.
